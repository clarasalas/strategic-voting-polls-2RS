Introduction

Do voters rally behind fewer candidates before the first round?

Only the top two of the first round reach the runoff. Voters whose favourite looks out of reach may switch to a candidate who can make it, and polls are how they know who can. This page follows every poll from 2002 to 2022 to see what they showed, and how far the result moved from them.

Polls are not forecasts: when the result differs, either the polls were off or voters changed their minds late, and these data cannot tell which.

Concentration

Do voters gather on fewer candidates?

Yes, at the very end. Between the final polls and election day, the vote gathered on fewer candidates in every election: barely in 2002 and 2017, clearly in 2007 and 2022. In the months before, it mostly spread out.

Why concentration changed · change in HHI

How it is measured

Concentration is the Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI): add up the squares of every candidate's share. Five candidates on 20% each give 0.20, two candidates on 50% give 0.50. The higher it is, the more the vote is gathered on a few candidates.

2002 stands apart: its result was as spread out as if the vote had gone to almost nine equal candidates, against about five in the other elections.

Two ways the vote can concentrate

Voters can move to the bigger candidates, or candidates can drop out and leave their voters to others. Both make the vote more concentrated, but they are not the same thing: in the first case voters coordinate, in the second, parties and candidates do it for them.

To tell them apart, concentration is also computed on a fixed group of candidates, those who won at least 2% of the vote. That group is the same in every poll and in the result, so a change there comes from voters moving. The rest comes from candidates dropping out.

What it shows

During the campaign, from the first polls about eight months before to the final two weeks, voters spread out rather than gathered, in every election but 2022. The rises in concentration in 2012 and 2022 came from candidates dropping out.

From the final polls to the vote, nobody drops out any more: the gathering is voters moving, strongest in 2022, then 2007.

Concentration

Does a rising candidate keep rising?

Mostly, yes. Candidates rising in the polls tended to keep rising, and falling ones to keep falling, right up to election day.

How to read it

Each dot is a candidate polling at least 3% a week before the vote. Across: how much their poll average moved from five weeks to one week before. Up: how much more they moved between the last week of polls and the result.

Dots along the dashed line mean the movement carried on. Dots near the horizontal line mean it stopped.

What it shows

For every point a candidate gained in the month before the last week, they gained about 0.6 more by election day, on average. Late movement does not reverse: it carries on after the last polls.

Over the whole campaign the same holds, more weakly: a gain over three weeks was followed by about 0.4 times as much over the next three (95% interval 0.29 to 0.53).

How the races unfolded

The campaigns

Each line is a candidate's average across the polls of the moment, over the eight months before the vote. Hover a line to see that candidate's polls; the dot at the right end is their actual result.

Polls testing every candidate who ended up on the ballot: used for the lines Other polls: left out

How to read it

Dashed lines: candidates who were polled but did not run in the end. Labels at the top: a candidate replacing others from their party in the polls, or dropping out; hover them for details.

Grey area: the final weeks, once the official list of candidates is out. Pale stretches: few polls at the time, so the average is less reliable.

Why some lines start late or break off

Only polls that test every candidate who ended up on the ballot are used (dark bars). A poll without, say, Zemmour would hand his share to the others and make the lines jump.

Early in some campaigns, such polls are rare or missing: in 2002 before February, in 2022 in autumn 2021. A gap in a line means there were none at the time.

How the races unfolded

The race for second place

Only the top two of the first round go through to the runoff. In 2007, 2012 and 2022, every poll of the final two weeks had the right two on top. In 2002, none did: Le Pen beat Jospin to second place by 0.7 points.

How to read it

Top: each candidate's place in the poll average. The green band is the two places that qualify; the dashed line marks the day from which the top two in the polls no longer changed.

Bottom: the gap between second and third place in the polls, for every election. The closer to zero, the more the second runoff place was a toss-up.

What it shows

When second and third are far apart, a poll error of a point or two changes nothing, and the polls show the final pair months ahead: about 200 days in 2007 and 2012. When they are close, as in 2002, 2017 and 2022, the same small error can send the wrong candidate through.

How accurate the polls were

Who gains on election day?

The candidate in third place. In all five elections, the third-placed candidate in the final polls did at least as well on election day, by 2.6 points on average. The candidate in fourth usually did worse.

How to read it

Left: for the selected election, how much better (above zero) or worse each candidate did than the final two weeks of polls said. Outlined: second and third place in those polls.

Right: every candidate of the five elections, grouped by their place in the final polls; the black bar is the average, the arrows count who did better ↑ or worse ↓.

What it shows

This is what you would expect if some voters switched at the last moment to a candidate who could still reach the runoff, away from one who could not.

But polls that underestimated the third-placed candidate every time would produce the same picture, and these data cannot tell which it is.

How far were the polls from the result?

In the final two weeks, a single poll typically missed a candidate's result by 0.9 points in 2017, and by 2.4 points in 2022.

Each poll of the final two weeks · typical miss per candidate, in points

How to read it

Each dot is one poll: how far it was from the result on a typical candidate (the square root of the average squared gap, over the candidates on the ballot). Hover a dot for the candidate it missed most.

Black bar: the average over polls. Ring: the same measure for the average of all those polls.

What it shows

Averaging all the polls does barely better than a single one: 0.8 points in 2017, 2.3 in 2022. Most of the gap is shared by every poll, whether because they all leaned the same way or because voters moved after the last polls.

Polls of one election miss together, so the five elections describe five campaigns; they do not make a trend.

How accurate the polls were

Polling companies compared

Companies differ little. They usually put a candidate within a point of the other polls, and on average their final poll was within about 0.3 points of that year's typical accuracy.

Each company's final poll · typical miss per candidate, against the average of that year's final polls

How to read it

Top: for the selected election, how much higher (red) or lower (blue) each company put a candidate than the other polls taken at the same time, on average. Faint cells: too small to tell from chance.

Bottom: each company's last poll before each election, one dot per election. Left of zero: closer to the result than that year's average final poll. Companies with a final poll in at least three elections.

What it shows

Harris Interactive and Ipsos were slightly closer to the result than the others, Kantar and CSA slightly further, by a few tenths of a point.

These gaps are small next to those between elections, rest on three to five elections per company, and with eight companies compared, one or two can stand out by chance. Taking each company's last poll keeps the comparison fair: they all end within 2 to 10 days of the vote, and correcting for that changes almost nothing.

The data. The polls come from the French Wikipedia lists of polls, checked against two other collections (france-politique.fr for 2002, NSPPolls for 2022) and against the pollsters' own reports: 693 polls, 136 corrections. Polls repeated daily on overlapping samples are counted once.

The lines average the polls around each day, giving more weight to the closest ones (over about ten days). Where polls are few, the window widens and the line turns pale; every figure quoted on this page uses the fixed window. Only polls that test every candidate who ended up on the ballot are used. Dates are the last day of fieldwork, or the publication date in 2007 and 2012.

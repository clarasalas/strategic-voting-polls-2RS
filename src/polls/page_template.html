French presidential elections · 2002–2022

What the polls said before the first round

Every first-round poll of five presidential elections, set against what happened on election day: how each race unfolded, who seemed headed for the runoff, and how far the polls were from the result.

A poll measures how people say they will vote on the day it is taken; it is not a forecast. When the result differs from the last polls, either the polls were off, or voters changed their minds at the last minute, for instance to help a candidate reach the runoff. These data cannot tell the two apart.

2 · Who reaches the runoff

The race that matters is for second place

Only the top two of the first round go through to the runoff. In 2007, 2012 and 2022, every poll of the final two weeks had the right two candidates on top. In 2002, none did: Le Pen beat Jospin to second place by 0.7 points.

When second and third place are far apart, a poll error of a point or two changes nothing, and the polls show the final pair months ahead. When they are close, as in 2002, 2017 and 2022, the same small error can send the wrong candidate through.

3 · The last weeks

On election day, the third-placed candidate gains

In all five elections, the candidate in third place in the final polls did at least as well on election day, by 2.6 points on average. The candidate in fourth usually did worse.

This is what you would expect if some voters switched at the last moment to a candidate who could still reach the runoff, away from one who could not. But polls that underestimated the third-placed candidate every time would produce the same picture, and these data cannot tell which it is.

How accurate was each poll?

In the final two weeks, a single poll typically missed a candidate's result by 0.9 points in 2017 and by 2.4 points in 2022. The average of all those polls did barely better, 0.8 and 2.3 points: most of the gap is shared by every poll, whether because they all leaned the same way or because voters moved after the last polls. Five elections describe five campaigns; they do not make a trend.

Each dot is one poll of the final two weeks: how far it was from the result on a typical candidate (the square root of the average squared gap, over the candidates on the ballot). Black bar: the average over polls. Ring: the same measure for the average of all those polls.

4 · Concentration

Voters gather on fewer candidates at the very end

Between the final polls and election day, the vote gathered on fewer candidates in every election: barely in 2002 and 2017, clearly in 2007 and 2022. Over the months before, it mostly spread out.

Concentration is measured with the Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI): add up the squares of every candidate's share. Five candidates on 20% each give 0.20, two candidates on 50% give 0.50. The higher it is, the more the vote is gathered on a few candidates. 2002 stands apart, with a vote as spread out as if it had gone to almost nine equal candidates, against about five in the other elections.

Why concentration changed · change in HHI

The vote can concentrate for two reasons: voters move to the bigger candidates, or candidates drop out of the race. To tell them apart, concentration is also computed on the same group of candidates all along, those who won at least 2%: changes there are voters moving; the rest comes from candidates dropping out. During the campaign: from the first polls, about eight months before, to the final two weeks. From the final polls to the vote: from the last two weeks of polls to the result.

5 · Pollsters and momentum

Pollsters mostly agree, and movement carries on

Polling companies usually differ by less than a point on a candidate. And candidates who were rising in the polls tended to keep rising, those falling to keep falling, right up to election day.

Over the campaign as a whole, a candidate who gained a point over three weeks gained about 0.4 more over the next three, on average.

Does a rising candidate keep rising?

Each dot is a candidate polling at least 3%. Across: how much their poll average moved from five weeks to one week before the vote. Up: how much more they moved between the last week of polls and the result. Dots along the dashed line mean the movement carried on: for every point gained in the month before, about 0.6 more on election day, on average.

The data. The polls come from the French Wikipedia lists of polls, checked against two other collections (france-politique.fr for 2002, NSPPolls for 2022) and against the pollsters' own reports: 693 polls, 136 corrections. Polls repeated daily on overlapping samples are counted once.

The lines average the polls around each day, giving more weight to the closest ones (over about ten days). Where polls are few, the window widens and the line turns pale; every figure quoted on this page uses the fixed window. Only polls that test every candidate who ended up on the ballot are used: a poll without, say, Zemmour would give his share to the others and make the lines jump. Such polls appear as light bars under the first chart; early in some campaigns (2002 before February, 2022 in autumn 2021) they are all there is, which is why some lines start late or break off. Dates are the last day of fieldwork, or the publication date in 2007 and 2012.
